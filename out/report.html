The gap is in the lap times. Summing a car's lap times gives its elapsed race time at the end of every lap, so the difference between two cars' sums is the gap between them. Exact, no interpolation, no telemetry. Everything here rests on that one observation.

Where the pit-lane time lands

In this schema a stop recorded on lap L puts 2.85 s of excess on lap L and 19.21 s on lap L+1 — the pit-lane time is mostly in the out-lap. So on the lap the chased car pits, the chasing car still reads -17.0 s: a pit loss not yet paid, not an undercut that failed. Every horizon below is therefore counted from one lap after the second stop, which is the first point at which both cars have paid the same toll.

The control is not zero. A car that is behind is usually behind because it is slower, and in these windows it loses about two seconds to the car ahead even when neither gains any tyre advantage. Measured against zero the undercut looks worthless; measured against the control it is worth two to four seconds, best at a head start of 3 laps.

Does the gain run through tyre age?

The pure-pace model says the whole gain is the rival's tyre age times the head start, with no free term for the head start on its own: on each advantage lap the chasing car is quicker by exactly the age penalty, and that is the only channel. Fitting both terms tests it.

The free head-start term is indistinguishable from zero, so the form the physics implies is not rejected and the gain can be pushed entirely through the age channel — which turns its coefficient into an estimate of the tyre-age penalty itself.

A second opinion on tyre degradation

Two estimators of the same quantity, built from different features of the same season. One regresses a car's lap times on its own tyre age. The other reads the pace difference between two cars, one on fresh tyres and one not, over a handful of laps.

The gap-based estimate is 1.30× the lap-time one, which is the direction a tyre cliff predicts — a linear fit through stints that ended before the tyre went away understates the penalty. But the intervals overlap, so this is agreement between two methods rather than a contradiction, and one season cannot resolve the difference.

Read later and later, the implied penalty decays to nothing. That is exactly the pure-pace prediction: once the rival has stopped, the chasing car is the one on the older set, and it hands the gain back at the same rate it won it. On pace alone, an undercut buys nothing permanent.

Half the raw effect was composition. Without controls, getting ahead appears to be worth +7.14 s of avoided payback. But pairs that end up ahead started about three seconds apart and pairs that do not started about twelve, and a pair that starts close is a pair of cars of similar pace. With the starting gap in the model the figure is +4.14 s — real, highly significant, and about half what the raw comparison suggested.
